\title{Reading Is Reproducible, Adjudicating Is Not Portable:\\
Auditing Cross-Paper Comparisons in Robust RAG}
\author{Anonymous Authors}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Robust retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) papers share labels such as noise
robustness, faithfulness, security and calibration while answering different
decision questions. We ask what can be reliably established about a cross-paper
comparison, and what cannot. On a source-verified ledger, \LedgerStatedUndef{}
of the \LedgerStated{} comparisons an author actually stated are undefined and
\LedgerStatedNarrow{} narrow to a matched operating point; in the sharpest case
one attack is reported at $1\%$ by the paper that benchmarks it and at $84.2\%$
by its own authors on the same dataset and retriever, because one number counts
answers changed and the other counts passages retrieved. Can such an audit be automated? A deterministic
screen over \FrameScreened{} full texts yields \FrameCandidates{} candidates,
coded independently by three judges from three developers. Whether a number was
\emph{transported} from another paper or \emph{re-implemented} here is recovered
reliably ($AC_1$ \DecProv{}, $\kappa$ \DecProvK{}); whether the comparison
\emph{survives} is not (\DecVerdict{}, \DecVerdictK{}). A fully crossed pool of
\TierPanelN{} judges---three checkpoints from each of three developers---shows
this is not one weak judge: an all-frontier panel reaches only
\TierFrontierVerdictK{}, and two frontier checkpoints from \emph{different}
developers reach \TierCompFFCross{} against \TierCompFFSame{} for two from the
same one. Adjudication does not port. The five
coordinates are themselves \emph{not} a reliable instrument---inter-judge
$\kappa$ runs \DecCoordKMin{}--\DecCoordKMax{}, and they lose to a
majority-class baseline---so we state them as disclosure fields, not a score.
Pricing the
measurement gap needs between-reviewer heterogeneity $\sigma_r$; three coders
leave it at $[0.00,\SigmaDiagHi]$, so we re-estimate it on five public crossed
corpora and \SigmaPanelReviewers{} machine reviewers, obtaining
\SigmaMin{}--\SigmaMax{}, across which a source-only test rejects a true null at
least \SimMinSize\% of the time. We release the frame, all codings, two
verifiers and every simulation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Retrieval-augmented generation conditions a generator on retrieved evidence
\citep{lewis2020rag,dpr2020,realm2020}, and robust-RAG studies ask what happens
when that evidence is irrelevant, insufficient, conflicting, stale, weakly
sourced, or malicious. Two reported ``robustness'' gains may nevertheless
concern different retrieval snapshots, attacker capabilities, interventions,
outcome units, or deployment actions. Source verification is therefore
necessary but insufficient: a cross-paper claim must also establish that both
results answer a compatible decision question.

\paragraph{An answer, in one case.}
PoisonedRAG reports that it ``could achieve a 97\% ASR by injecting 5 malicious
texts for each target question'' on Natural Questions, and its Table~4 places
that beside four baselines, the weakest of which---the Corpus Poisoning Attack
of \citet{poisoningRetrievalCorpora2023}---is recorded at $0.01$
\citep{poisonedRAG2024}. A reader carries away that corpus poisoning does not
work. On the same dataset and the same retriever, its own authors report it
placing an adversarial passage in the top-20 for $84.2\%$ of held-out queries
with a \emph{single} such passage, and $99.4\%$ with fifty.

Neither number is wrong, and PoisonedRAG says so itself: the baseline is
ineffective, it writes, because it ``cannot achieve the generation condition.''
That is the whole content of the $0.01$. PoisonedRAG's outcome is the fraction
of target questions answered with the attacker's chosen answer; corpus
poisoning does not choose an answer, and its own paper defines success as ``the
percentage of queries for which at least one adversarial passage is retrieved in
the top-$k$ results.'' Scoring a retrieval attack on an answer-manipulation
metric is exactly what a baseline table is for, and inside PoisonedRAG's harness
the $1\%$ is the right number.

What it does not license is the sentence a reader forms from it. The two figures
are printed in different units in their own papers, a fraction of target
questions against a percentage of queries; they count different events; and even
the retrieved set they range over differs, PoisonedRAG's context being top-5
where the retrieval metric is top-20. The outcome coordinate (C4) is unmatched,
no common scope exists in either text, and the comparison is undefined rather
than repairable: there is no operating point at which $1\%$ and $84.2\%$ answer
one question. What survives is the negative statement---these two attacks cannot
be ranked by these two numbers---and, usefully, the observation that the paper
being benchmarked said as much in a sentence the number outlived.

\paragraph{A correction to a previous version.}
An earlier version of this paper opened with a different case, and it was
wrong. It stated that RobustRAG introduces its certificate as reducing
PoisonedRAG's attack success ``from 97\%'' \citep{certifiedRAG2024}. RobustRAG
writes ``from over 90\%'', attributes the 90+\% figure to prompt injection
rather than to PoisonedRAG, and re-implements the poisoning attack in its own
harness rather than quoting any number from it. The only ``97'' in RobustRAG is
its own prompt-injection rate for Llama2-C7B on RQA; we had matched it to
PoisonedRAG's numerically identical but unrelated $0.97$. We record the
corrected entry in the ledger, and we extended the released verifier so that
every string this paper prints inside quotation marks as coming from a source
is checked verbatim against that source's line range---the check whose absence
let the error through, since the old verifier tested only numerals against
provenance ranges and never tested prose. \S\ref{sec:cases} states what the
case does show.


\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i) A five-coordinate comparability audit with a source-verified ledger of
twelve cross-paper comparisons. Of the \LedgerStated{} an author actually
stated, \LedgerStatedUndef{} are undefined and \LedgerStatedNarrow{} narrow to
a matched operating point; the remaining \LedgerInvited{} are juxtapositions a
reader is led to make and no author committed. Every quoted value, every quoted
string, and every number the manuscript asserts about its own artifacts is
machine-checked by three released verifiers whose coverage boundary we state
(\S\ref{sec:cases}). (ii) A census that asks whether the audit can be
automated: a deterministic frame of \FrameCandidates{} candidates from all
\FrameScreened{} full texts, coded independently by three judges from three
developers, plus a tier-crossed control panel of \TierPanelN{} judges that
separates ``property of the task'' from ``one weak judge''
(\S\ref{sec:census}). The split is by kind of question, not by difficulty:
provenance is reliable under both $AC_1$ and $\kappa$, the verdict is reliable
under neither. (iii) The negative result that follows: the five coordinates are
not a coding instrument. Independent judges code them at $\kappa$
\DecCoordKMin{}--\DecCoordKMax{}, and against human adjudication they lose to a
majority-class baseline. We therefore state them as disclosure fields and
withdraw any claim that they can be scored. (iv) A fully crossed panel of
\CMJmodels{} machine claim-reviewers from \CMJdevelopers{} developers, which
supplies the between-reviewer heterogeneity \S\ref{sec:simulation} needs and
shows that an instruction effect estimated on one checkpoint does not transfer
(\S\ref{sec:controlled-models}). (v) A reporting audit of the nine robust-RAG
papers that collect human judgments, a multi-anchor estimate of $\sigma_r$ from
seven crossed designs, and a simulation over the resulting range identifying
two retrospectively executable remedies (\S\ref{sec:simulation}).

\section{Positioning}
\label{sec:related}

Architectures differ in where retrieval enters and how evidence is fused
\citep{drqa2017,knnlm2020,fid2021,retro2022,atlas2023,replug2024}, and
evaluation, trustworthiness, security, conflict and abstention surveys expose
different failure surfaces \citep{ragSurvey2024,ragEvaluationSurvey2024,
trustRAGCompass2024,securityRAGSurvey2026,xuKnowledgeConflicts2024,
wenAbstentionSurvey2025}. Common-harness comparisons improve empirical control
\citep{ragBestPractices2024,alushiRAGComparison2026} but do not resolve whether
a quoted cross-paper result concerns the same evidence state, capability
envelope, outcome or action. Our unit is an individual cross-paper
juxtaposition, and our output is a per-comparison verdict with a
coordinate-matched number where one is recoverable (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:main-prior-work-comparison}).

Auditing how human evaluations are reported is established
\citep{howcroft2020twenty,vanderlee2021human,belz2021systematic,
belz2023impossibility,clark2021human}; our audit is smaller and claims no
prevalence those studies do not already establish. Annotation research
separately distinguishes reproducibility from truth
\citep{artstein2008intercoder,dawid1979observer}, treats disagreement as signal
\citep{uma2021disagreement}, warns that majority voting erases structured
variation \citep{plank2022labelvariation,davani2022disagreements}, and models
rater and item effects \citep{passonneau2014annotation,paun2018bayesian};
crossed designs require both sources of variation \citep{baayen2008crossed},
clustering follows treatment assignment \citep{abadie2023clustering}, and power
belongs to the randomized unit
\citep{card2020power,vanmiltenburg2021preregistering}.

\paragraph{Analyzing at the wrong unit.}
That inference over the wrong factor inflates type-I error is not new:
\citet{clark1973language} named the language-as-fixed-effect fallacy and
\citet{judd2012stimuli} gave the crossed random-effects remedy for stimuli, and
both subsume the general form of our simulation. We claim three things they do
not supply. The parameter governing the inflation for claim review, $\sigma_r$,
has never been estimated for this task; we estimate it from several crossed
designs and report a range (\S\ref{sec:sigma}). The two remedies we evaluate
are chosen for being executable \emph{retrospectively}. And the artifact both
need is absent from every robust-RAG paper that collects human judgments, which
turns a known statistical point into a concrete reporting requirement.

\section{The Two-Layer Audit}
\label{sec:estimands}

Four boundaries motivate the coordinates: evidence availability is not evidence
use \citep{irrelevantContext2023,raat2024,rgb2023,chainOfNote2023}; support,
authority and conflict are distinct, so a response can be correct but
unsupported or faithful to a superseded source
\citep{ragTruth2024,astuteRAG2025,freshllms2024}; natural retrieval failure and
adaptive attack are different games under different access, budget and
persistence assumptions \citep{poisonedRAG2024,phantom2024,certifiedRAG2024};
and a confidence signal is not an action policy
\citep{guo2017calibration,geifman2017selective,selfRAG2023,crag2024,
sufficientContext2024}. Appendix~\ref{sec:robust-rag-landscape} develops these
with paper-level contrasts.

The substantive layer records five coordinates on each side. So that the body
is readable without the appendix, they are, in full:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt,parsep=0pt]
\item[\textbf{C1}] \emph{Evidence state} --- what is observable at the decision
boundary: supported, distracting, insufficient, conflicting, stale or weakly
sourced, adversarially manipulated, or not evidence-conditioned at all;
together with the corpus snapshot, retriever, top-$k$ and whether a gold
passage is present.
\item[\textbf{C2}] \emph{Origin / capability} --- how that state arose. Benign
retrieval error, a constructed stressor, natural drift, or an adversary; and
for an adversary, the access, budget, persistence, and crucially whether the
budget counts documents \emph{injected into the corpus} or documents
\emph{retrieved into the context}, and whether the metric runs over all queries
or only triggered ones.
\item[\textbf{C3}] \emph{Intervention} --- which part of the system changes:
corpus or index, retriever, reranker, context assembly, generator, verifier,
system control, or the evaluation artifact.
\item[\textbf{C4}] \emph{Outcome and unit} --- what object is measured and in
what unit: answer correctness, retrieval quality, claim support, attack
success, leakage, calibration, selective risk or cost; per question, per
response or per span.
\item[\textbf{C5}] \emph{Policy / utility} --- which response is selected under
which costs: answer, verify, re-retrieve, expose conflict, clarify, quarantine,
recover or abstain, with the error, abstention and follow-up prices stated.
\end{itemize}

\noindent A claim-specific control check accompanies them (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:main-framework-overview}); these specialize distinctions from
evidence synthesis, selective prediction and security evaluation
\citep{richardson1995pico,chow1970reject,papernot2018sok}. The measurement
layer asks how judgments about those coordinates were produced: which people,
items, assignments, references and downstream losses define the target. Passing
one layer cannot repair failure in the other.

\paragraph{What these fields are, and are not.}
They are \emph{disclosure} fields. \S\ref{sec:census} shows that independent
judges cannot code them reliably ($\kappa$ \DecCoordKMin{}--\DecCoordKMax{})
and that against human adjudication they lose to a majority-class baseline, so
we make no claim that a comparison can be scored on them. What survives that
result is weaker and still useful: whether a paper \emph{states} each
coordinate is checkable even when whether the coordinates \emph{match} is not,
and the worked cases of \S\ref{sec:cases} show that the failures which matter
are failures to state.

\paragraph{Decision rule.}
Record each coordinate on both sides from the sources alone. If every
coordinate matches, the quoted gap stands. If one differs but a side reports an
operating point at the other's setting, re-read there and report the matched
number. If one differs and no matched operating point exists, the gap is
undefined and the comparison must be replaced by the common scope both studies
support, or dropped---never by a substitute number computed across the
unmatched coordinate. Three targets are routinely conflated in the measurement
layer: a \emph{panel contrast} between two realized panels, a \emph{prompt
effect} over a reviewer and item population, and \emph{expected downstream
loss}. They coincide only under assumptions that should be stated rather than
inherited from the aggregation rule (Appendix
\ref{sec:estimand-formalization}); in particular, adding items with the panel
fixed improves conditional precision without creating new prompt assignments,
so it cannot repair a reviewer-level claim.

\section{Twelve Worked Comparisons}
\label{sec:cases}

We assembled comparisons of two kinds from \FrameScreened{} locally extracted
full texts. \emph{Stated} comparisons (\LedgerStated{}) are ones an author made:
the paper names another system and asserts a relation to it.
\emph{Survey-invited} juxtapositions (\LedgerInvited{}) are ones a survey's
grouping leads a reader to make and no author committed; we never call one a
quoted claim, and we report them separately throughout. Within the stated
comparisons a further distinction matters, and reviewer feedback on an earlier
version is what forced it into the ledger: a paper may \emph{transport} the
other paper's number, may \emph{re-implement} the other system and print its
own number, or may assert a relation while supplying \emph{no} number for the
other side. These are different claims and they fail differently.

Every value is copied verbatim with a file and line range, and three released
checks run over the result. \texttt{verify\_cross\_paper\_cases.py} re-opens
each range and fails on any numeral that is not present at the cited lines.
Because an earlier version of this paper attributed a quotation to a paper that
does not contain it, \texttt{verify\_manuscript\_quotations.py} additionally
requires every string this manuscript prints in quotation marks---in the prose
\emph{and} in every table it includes---to occur verbatim at a registered
source line range, with scare-quoted category labels declared as such. And
because an earlier version reported a difference computed on a different
population from the numbers it appeared to be a difference of,
\texttt{verify\_prose\_artifact\_consistency.py} checks that every quantity the
manuscript asserts about its own artifacts equals the artifact field that
produced it, and that each reported difference is the subtraction of two
numbers printed in the same table. All \VerifChecks{} ledger checks pass, as do
the manuscript-quotation and prose-consistency suites. We state the coverage
boundary explicitly: these checks establish that our quotations and numbers are
faithful to their sources and to our own artifacts. They do not establish that
our \emph{judgments} are right, and \S\ref{sec:census} is the evidence that
independent judges would not reproduce them.

\paragraph{What the ledger shows.}
Of the \LedgerStated{} stated comparisons, \LedgerStatedUndef{} are undefined,
\LedgerStatedNarrow{} narrow to a matched operating point, and
\LedgerStatedSurv{} survive unchanged; of the \LedgerInvited{} survey-invited
juxtapositions, \LedgerInvitedUndef{} are undefined. We report narrowing as a
\emph{success} of the rule, not a failure of the comparison: \S\ref{sec:estimands}
says to re-read at the matched operating point, and a narrowed comparison is one
where that worked. All five security cases involve an unmatched attacker
capability or objective. ``Noise robustness'' names at least two measurements,
so RGB's substring-match $76.00\%$ and RAAT's $71.47$ exact match differ both in
metric strictness and in what counts as noise, leaving the $4.53$-point gap
without a determined sign. The audit does not flag everything: CRAG's
$6.9$-point gap over Self-RAG at a shared backbone, retriever, dataset and
metric stands, AstuteRAG's $4.4$-point accuracy gap over RobustRAG stands inside
AstuteRAG's own harness, and two of three within-harness controls pass
unchanged. This ledger is single-coded by us, which \S\ref{sec:census} addresses
directly.

\paragraph{A claim with no transported number.}
RobustRAG certifies $38.0\%$ accuracy on RQA at $k=10$ retrieved passages
against $k'=1$ malicious retrieved passage, and states that it ``demonstrates
strong empirical robustness against PoisonedRAG [45] and indirect prompt
injection attacks [16], reducing attack success rates from over 90\% to
approximately 10\%'' \citep{certifiedRAG2024}. Placed beside PoisonedRAG's
headline $0.97$, that sentence invites a defense-beats-attack reading. Three
things block it, and none is a mis-citation. RobustRAG transports no number: it
re-implements the attack at a strengthened configuration, repeating malicious
content ``10 times for QA and 3 times for the long-generation task \dots\ in a
single passage''. Its own vanilla-RAG poisoning rates are $80.0$, $37.0$, $86.0$
(Mistral) and $76.0$, $38.0$, $44.0$ (Llama2), so ``over 90\%'' is not attained
by the poisoning attack at all---only by the prompt-injection column ($66.0$,
$85.0$, $100$, $97.0$, $93.0$, $98.0$), which is what its results section
actually says. And the certificate is a third quantity again, binding where
PoisonedRAG's own Table~17 reports $0.48$. Three numbers, $0.97$, $0.86$ and
$0.48$, answer three different questions; the case is undefined, and under the
provenance taxonomy of \S\ref{sec:census} it is the rare stratum that asserts a
relation without supplying the other side's number.

\subsection{A comparison our own rule discards}
\label{sec:discarded}

The most arresting number the ledger produces is one we do not report as a
result. Surveys group PoisonedRAG and Phantom under ``RAG poisoning'', inviting
a reading of PoisonedRAG's $97\%$ against Phantom's $93.3\%$---a $3.7$-point
lead that a budget-matched re-reading would turn into a $45.3$-point deficit,
since PoisonedRAG's rate at one malicious text among the retrieved passages is
$0.48$. Three things forbid reporting that reversal. The budgets are not the
same kind: PoisonedRAG's $0.48$ is conditional on one malicious text being
\emph{among the top-5 retrieved}, while Phantom's $93.3\%$ follows from one
document \emph{injected into the corpus}, and injection does not condition on
retrieval succeeding. Phantom's rate is measured only on trigger-carrying
queries and PoisonedRAG's on arbitrary target questions, so the evaluation
populations differ. And the objectives differ---induced refusal against a
targeted answer---so C4 is unmatched with no common unit. By our own rule that
makes the comparison undefined, and an undefined comparison may not be replaced
by a signed substitute. We record it as \texttt{undefined} with no matched
number and state the negative conclusion---these two attack results cannot be
ordered---rather than the arithmetic.

\section{What a Census Can and Cannot Decide}
\label{sec:census}

The worked ledger shows the failure exists but cannot say how common it is, and
it is coded by us. So we built the screen an author or reviewer would actually
want---one that finds candidate comparisons and adjudicates them---and measured
how much of it works.

\paragraph{Frame and coding.}
A deterministic screen over all \FrameScreened{} full texts marks a window as a
candidate when it names a gazetteer system absent from that paper's own title
and abstract region, contains at least two values formatted as results, and
matches one of three declared strata: baseline-table language, explicit
attribution of a number to another paper, or a survey-style score table.
Nothing in the screen is a judgment, and the strata are defined by document
features, never by the audit outcome; the gazetteer, cues and number pattern
are released. It returns \FrameCandidates{} candidates in \FrameFiles{} papers,
and this frame---not a selection from it---is what we code. Three judges served
by three developers---\texttt{gpt-5-mini}, \texttt{claude-opus-4.6} and
\texttt{gemini-3.1-pro-preview}---code every candidate independently from the
same passage plus $\pm45$ lines of context, the same codebook and a
seed-shuffled order, never seeing one another's output. \CensusCoded{} of the
\FrameCandidates{} candidates returned a parseable coding from all three and
are the ones analysed; the remainder failed to parse for at least one judge and
are neither dropped selectively nor counted. One of the three is a
mini-tier model; we name the checkpoints here rather than calling the panel
``frontier'', and the control panel below is what licenses any claim that the
result is not about that judge. Each returns a rationale first, then
eligibility, the two sides, a label per coordinate, a verdict, a matched
number, and a verbatim evidence quote we check mechanically
(\CensusQuoteRate\% are found in the passage shown). A second pass asks one
further question of every eligible candidate: was the other paper's number
\emph{transported} here, or \emph{re-implemented} inside this harness? Labels
combine only by mechanical majority. The codebook was revised once on a
declared \CensusDevSize{}-candidate development pass, released separately;
results below are the \CensusHeldout{} held-out candidates.

\paragraph{Result: the split is by kind of question, not by difficulty.}
\CensusEligible{} candidates from \CensusPapers{} papers are eligible by
majority, and Table~\ref{tab:cross-paper-census} reports every field under both
Gwet's $AC_1$ and Fleiss' $\kappa$. Reporting both is not decoration. $AC_1$ is
the right choice when marginals are skewed, which they are here, but it rewards
prevalence, and on this frame the two statistics disagree about which findings
are real. We follow $\kappa$ wherever they disagree.

One descriptive judgment is reliable under both. \emph{How the other number was
obtained}---transported from the other paper or re-implemented in this
harness---reaches $AC_1$ \DecProv{} and $\kappa$ \DecProvK{}. The inferential
judgment is reliable under neither: \emph{does the comparison survive} reaches
$AC_1$ \DecVerdict{} and $\kappa$ \DecVerdictK{}. The difference between them is
\DecGap{} \DecGapCI{} under $AC_1$ and \DecGapK{} \DecGapKCI{} under $\kappa$,
with both statistics computed on the same items by the same judges, every
interval resampling \emph{source papers}, and
\texttt{share\_above\_zero}$=1.000$ over \DecDraws{} draws in each case.

The eligibility judgment does \emph{not} support the same claim, and an earlier
version of this paper reported it as though it did. Eligibility reaches $AC_1$
\DecElig{} over all \CensusHeldout{} candidates; recomputed on the
majority-eligible subset where the verdict is defined it reaches \DecEligM{},
and the difference from the verdict is \DecGapEl{} there. But that rise is
selection, not skill---the subset is chosen for having produced agreeing
eligibility labels---and under $\kappa$ the same quantity is \DecEligMK{} and
the difference \emph{reverses} to \DecGapElK{} \DecGapElKCI{}. We therefore rest
the descriptive-versus-inferential claim on provenance alone, and we report
eligibility twice, over both populations, so that each difference in the table
is a subtraction of two numbers printed in that table. Calling $AC_1=\DecElig{}$
``substantial'' would also misuse a term of art: on the Landis--Koch scale that
range is \emph{moderate}.

\paragraph{The five coordinates are not a coding instrument.}
This is the census's sharpest negative result and it runs against our own
proposal. Under $AC_1$ the coordinates look serviceable, \DecCoordMin{} to
\DecCoordMax{}. Under $\kappa$ they run \DecCoordKMin{} to \DecCoordKMax{}, with
C3 \emph{below chance}. Two independent judges reading the same passage under
the same codebook do not agree on which coordinate is mismatched, and the higher
$AC_1$ values are prevalence artifacts: C3 is labelled ``mismatch'' on
$99.6\%$ of items, which is almost a constant. The human evidence points the
same way. Against adjudicated human coding the full rule reaches $.500$ exact
agreement, \emph{below} the $.537$ from predicting the modal verdict, and adding
C2 and C5 shifts exact agreement by $.000$ (95\% CI $[-.066,+.079]$, exact
McNemar $p=1.0$; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:rule-baselines}). We therefore make no
claim that the coordinates can be scored, by a model or by a person. They are
disclosure fields: things an author should state, whose \emph{absence} is
checkable even when their adjudication is not.

\paragraph{A control for judge capability.}
The panel above is one judge per developer, and one of the three is a
mini-tier model, so ``verdict disagreement is a property of the task'' is
confounded with ``one judge is weaker''. We therefore coded the entire frame
again with \TierNew{} further judges, giving a fully crossed pool of
\TierPanelN{}: a small checkpoint and two frontier checkpoints from each of
three developers. Every judge answers all \FrameCandidates{} windows under the
identical codebook, which yields \TierNPairs{} judge pairs and, for each
developer, a same-developer same-tier pair as a reference level.

\input{tables/tier_control}

\noindent Replacing the mini-tier judge helps, and does not rescue the verdict.
A frontier-only panel of \TierFrontierDevs{} developers reaches verdict
$\kappa$ \TierFrontierVerdictK{} against \TierSubmittedVerdictK{} for the
submitted panel---close to four times higher, and still only \emph{fair} on the
Landis--Koch scale. A small-tier panel of three developers reaches
\TierSmallVerdictK{}, which is chance.

The pairwise decomposition says where the variance lives, and it is not where a
pooled tier contrast would show it. Two frontier checkpoints from
\emph{different} developers agree at \TierCompFFCross{} on average, with a range
of \TierCompFFCrossLo{} to \TierCompFFCrossHi{}: some cross-developer frontier
pairs are worse than chance. Two small checkpoints from different developers
agree at \TierCompSSCross{}. Two frontier checkpoints from the \emph{same}
developer---nothing differing but the checkpoint---reach \TierCompFFSame{},
and that is the ceiling, attained at \TierSameDevVerdictK{}, \TierSameDevGoogleK{}
and \TierSameDevOpenAIK{} for the three developers. Capability tier therefore
matters, from chance to \TierCompFFCross{}, but a pooled ``same tier versus
different tier'' test is null (\TierTierDiff{}, $p=\TierTierP$) precisely
because ``same tier'' averages the small--small end with the
frontier--frontier end. Sharing a developer is worth \TierDevDiff{}
($p=\TierDevP$) over \TierNPairs{} pairs, which we report as the residual
structure rather than as an estimate.

That narrows the paper's claim rather than restoring it. ``Adjudicating is not
reproducible'' is too strong, and ``it is reproducible if you use good models''
is false: the best configuration we can build---frontier checkpoints, three
developers, one codebook, one passage---reaches \TierFrontierVerdictK{}, and the
only configurations that do better share a developer. The accurate statement is
that adjudication does not \emph{port}. Two checkpoints from one developer share
training data and alignment procedure, so their agreement bounds what correlated
judges achieve rather than what the task affords, and it is not evidence that a
well-specified verdict exists. What follows for practice is concrete: an author
cannot obtain a defensible verdict by asking one model family, upgrading the
model does not fix it, and a reported verdict rate is a property of the panel
that produced it.

\paragraph{What the frame is made of.}
Composition is reliable where verdicts are not, because provenance is the
judgment the judges agree on. Of \ProvClassified{} classified candidates,
\ProvReimplemented{} are re-implementations---the citing paper re-ran the other
system in its own corpus, retriever, generator and metric---and only
\ProvTransported{} transport a number across a harness boundary. Cross-paper
comparison here is therefore mostly a within-harness activity, so the failure
mode this paper is about has a narrow but identifiable habitat. Within it the
direction is clear and the estimate is not: transported comparisons fail
\ProvTransFail\% against \ProvReimplFail\% for re-implementations, and removing
any single source paper leaves the rates in \ProvTransLOO\% and
\ProvReimplLOO\%, but the transported stratum spans only \ProvTransPapers{}
papers with one contributing \ProvTransLargest\%, its paper-clustered interval
is \ProvTransFailCI\%, and a source-paper permutation test gives $p=.064$. We
report a direction, not an estimate, and decline to headline it.

\paragraph{What our human evidence is.}
\label{sec:development}
A 480-claim collection was designed to be the human study of this paper. One
returned batch contained shared response payloads, and a later export could not
be linked to controlled recruitment records, one-to-one pseudonym mappings and
separate response receipts. Because that internal audit trail was absent we
exclude every response, contrast, reference statistic, participant attribute and
timing claim derived from it. What remained was an 80-claim, four-contributor,
purposive sample that overlapped codebook development and had no recoverable
selection rule for 64 of its items, retained on the project owner's attestation
rather than on contributor-level records. An earlier version reported that
residue as a diagnostic subsection of its own, with six supporting appendix
tables; we have withdrawn all of it, because a venue cannot be asked to publish
a section on the understanding that a reader may treat it as empty. Two
findings from that collection are retained, and both cut \emph{against} us: the
majority-class baseline the coordinates fail to beat, above and in Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:rule-baselines}, and the three-coder $\hat\sigma_r$ in
\S\ref{sec:sigma}, which we report only to show that a three-rater design
cannot identify $\sigma_r$. No positive claim rests on either, and a reader who
declines the attestation can delete both without changing a conclusion. The
Ethics Statement gives the disclosure in full.

\section{A Crossed Panel of Claim Reviewers}
\label{sec:controlled-models}

Two questions remain that the published record cannot answer. How much do claim
reviewers differ from one another---the quantity \S\ref{sec:simulation} must
price? And does naming a coordinate change a decision? Both need a design the
literature does not supply: many reviewers, all judging the same items, against
a reference no reviewer produced. Language models are now routinely used as
claim judges, so a panel of them is a population of interest in its own right.

\paragraph{Benchmark, design, and gate.}
A deterministic benchmark holds 600 synthetic deployment decisions in 300
counterfactual pairs. In the \emph{C2} stratum (120 pairs) only whether the
deployed origin, access and budget fall inside an evaluated capability envelope
changes; half the pairs move both systems out and half only one, so a
capability shift can flip the ordering rather than always resolving into
deferral. In \emph{C5} (120 pairs) only the error-versus-abstention prices or
the exogenous price of a further study changes, with the deferral price
displayed as a constant, so deferring is optimal on 65 of 240 items. A
\emph{competence probe} (60 pairs) displays all three action losses and asks
for the minimum. Targets are the unique minimum of the displayed loss rule with
margin $\geq.02$, balanced across actions within every stratum. Each reviewer
answers every item under three arms crossed with four paraphrases, i.e.\
7{,}200 greedy decisions: \emph{ablated} names evidence state, intervention,
outcome, controls and action; \emph{full} adds explicit capability and utility
checks; \emph{placebo} adds generic reading instructions of matched length
naming neither, so \emph{full} minus \emph{placebo} is not confounded with
verbosity. Uncertainty is a bootstrap clustered on the 300 pairs and the grid
receives a Holm correction. A pre-declared gate admits a reviewer only if it
reaches 50\% on the probe in some arm and does not emit one action on over 90\%
of items. Frontier models violate the ``one action token'' instruction, so we
apply one normalisation to \emph{every} reviewer---where the strict parser
fails, the decision is the last standalone action token---which is symmetric by
construction and changes \CMJnormOpenChanged{} of the open checkpoints'
decisions against \CMJnormApiChanged\% of the API models'.

\input{tables/main_controlled_model_judges_v2}

\paragraph{The arm effect does not generalize.}
\CMJpassing{} of \CMJmodels{} reviewers passed the gate. On the
nine-checkpoint, Qwen-heavy sample of an earlier version the pooled \emph{full}
minus \emph{placebo} effect on the capability stratum was $+5.4$ points (95\%
CI $[+2.5,+8.2]$) and we read it as construct evidence. Over \CMJpassing{}
reviewers from \CMJdevelopers{} developers it is \CMJCtwoPooled{} points (95\%
CI [\CMJCtwoLow, \CMJCtwoHigh], $I^2=\CMJCtwoIsq\%$), and the utility stratum
gives \CMJCfivePooled{} (95\% CI [\CMJCfiveLow, \CMJCfiveHigh]), an
unambiguous null. Three summaries say why the capability mean is not a
population claim: the prediction interval for a newly drawn reviewer
\citep{higgins2009reintroduction} is [\CMJCtwoPIlow, \CMJCtwoPIhigh] and
contains zero; averaging within developer before pooling gives
\CMJCtwoDevPooled{} points (95\% CI [\CMJCtwoDevLow, \CMJCtwoDevHigh]), which
does not exclude zero; and per reviewer \CMJCtwoPos{} show a positive effect
whose interval excludes zero while \CMJCtwoNeg{} show a negative one. On the
competence probe, where naming a coordinate should not help, we report
equivalence rather than a non-significant $p$: against a pre-declared margin of
$\pm2$ points the effect is equivalent to zero
($p_{\mathrm{TOST}}=\CMJProbeTOST$) \citep{lakens2017equivalence}. We therefore
withdraw the construct claim the earlier version made.

\paragraph{What the panel does establish: reviewers are not interchangeable.}
The null on the arm contrast is not a null on the reviewer dimension.
Between-reviewer heterogeneity is enormous: $I^2$ is \CMJCtwoIsq\% on the
capability stratum, accuracy on the same 600 items ranges from \CMJprobeMin\%
to \CMJprobeMax\% across reviewers, and the between-reviewer ability spread on
the logit scale is $\hat\sigma_r=\SigmaPanel$
(Table~\ref{tab:sigma-r-anchors}), above the median of the five human corpora.
For a substantial minority of reviewers the \emph{sign} of the arm effect flips
with the wording of an instruction that is supposed to be about content
(paraphrase sign agreement $\le0.5$ for \CMJSignBelowHalf\% of
reviewer-by-stratum cells). Two consequences follow, and they are why this
section is in the paper. A panel of machine claim-reviewers is not
interchangeable, so the assignment-unit problem of \S\ref{sec:simulation}
applies to LLM-as-judge pipelines exactly as to human panels, with a larger
$\sigma_r$ than most human corpora we could find. And an instruction effect
estimated on one checkpoint, or on several from one developer, says almost
nothing about the next reviewer drawn.

\section{Checking Claims Is a Measurement Problem}
\label{sec:simulation}
\label{sec:published-audit}

\subsection{What published human evaluations report}

Of 39 venue-linked papers with local full text, a deterministic term screen
flagged 19 and full-text review retained nine that collect original human
judgments. Two reporting quantities must be kept apart and are routinely
conflated: the \emph{number of distinct annotators} is how many people
contributed any judgment, \emph{raters per item} is how many judged each item,
and only those two together with the \emph{item--annotator assignment}
determine the unit at which a treatment was randomized. Two of nine papers
report the number of distinct annotators; two report both raters per item and
the number of items; four report an agreement quantity; two report
compensation. None reports the item--annotator assignment, none releases
pre-aggregation votes, and none randomizes an audit prompt across annotators,
so this audit finds no \emph{verifiable} assignment-unit mismatch---only the
impossibility of checking for one. Absent fields are coded as reporting gaps,
not as evidence a practice was absent; the phenomenon itself is established at
far larger scale by \citet{howcroft2020twenty} and \citet{vanderlee2021human}.
The increment is that the missing fields are exactly the ones a reanalysis
needs. FaithEval reports three crowd workers per pair \citep{faithEval2025}; RAGTruth
two annotators per response and a third for large differences
\citep{ragTruth2024}; ParamMute six senior researchers and a multiply annotated
100-item subset but not their allocation to items \citep{paramMute2025}; PoisonedRAG
names the authors as evaluators without reviewer-level multiplicity
\citep{poisonedRAG2024}. In every case the released artifact is a final
aggregated label: enough for an agreement rate, not enough to recompute
uncertainty at the unit that was assigned.

\subsection{How heterogeneous are reviewers?}
\label{sec:sigma}

The consequence of analyzing at the wrong unit depends on $\sigma_r$, the
between-reviewer standard deviation of ability on the logit scale, identified
only by a design crossing reviewers with items. Our own crossed design is the
80-claim diagnostic, whose three coders each labelled all 80 claims against an
independent adjudication; a moment estimator removing the binomial component
gives $\hat\sigma_r=0.27$ with a reviewer-bootstrap 95\% interval of
$[0.00,\SigmaDiagHi]$. That interval is the problem, and it deserves stating
sharply rather than in a limitations paragraph: three raters give a variance two
degrees of freedom, the interval contains zero, and at $\sigma_r=0$ the
status-quo analysis is exactly calibrated, so from this design alone ``badly
miscalibrated'' and ``fine'' are indistinguishable. The literature cannot narrow
it either: a reported pairwise agreement constrains total latent dispersion but
not its split between reviewer and item components, so every $\sigma_r$ on a
$0$--$1.2$ grid reproduces the agreement two audited papers report, for some
item dispersion (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:reviewer-heterogeneity-anchor}).

\input{tables/sigma_r_anchors}

We therefore apply the same estimator to every crossed design we could obtain.
Five public human annotation corpora release per-annotator labels with stable
identifiers and cross annotators with items: LeWiDi MD-Agreement
\citep{leonardelli2023semeval}, Wikipedia Detox \citep{wulczyn2017detox},
GoEmotions \citep{demszky2020goemotions}, DynaSent \citep{potts2021dynasent}
and MultiPref \citep{miranda2024multipref}, spanning 76--3{,}944 annotators and
6{,}502--159{,}686 items. Each annotator is scored against a leave-one-out
majority of the \emph{other} annotators, so no rater is graded against a
reference containing their own vote. The panel of
\S\ref{sec:controlled-models} adds a crossed design of the kind now used to
review claims in practice, with a reference no reviewer produced. Every
\emph{large} crossed design places $\sigma_r$ between \SigmaMin{} and
\SigmaMax{} with a reviewer-bootstrap interval excluding zero; our own
three-coder design sits below that range at $0.27$ and does not exclude zero,
which is the point of including it. Three
qualifications. The human corpora are not claim review, so they establish the
order of magnitude of between-annotator ability variation rather than the value
for this task, and the abstract's simulation range is anchored on borrowed
parameters. A leave-one-out majority is noisier than an adjudicated reference,
which attenuates accuracies. And the interval reported is a \emph{reviewer}
bootstrap: an item bootstrap, which an earlier version reported, resamples the
wrong unit for a between-reviewer variance and is biased upward, which makes
excluding zero easier rather than harder. The conclusion is correspondingly
modest: $\sigma_r=0$ is not a live possibility for a realistic panel, and $0.27$
is a conservative choice within the anchored range.

\subsection{Design and results}

We simulate a two-arm claim-review study with twelve reviewers, six per prompt,
$G$ sources contributing six claims each, and a balanced three-of-six panel per
claim in both arms; baseline agreement is 75\% and source and claim logit
standard deviations are $.15$ and $.35$. Reviewer abilities are redrawn from
$N(0,\sigma_r^2)$ in every replicate, so the target is a reviewer-population
effect and the source-only analysis cannot be defended as answering a
realized-panel estimand instead (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:retrospective-remedies-full} repeats everything with abilities
at fixed quantiles). Each cell uses 5{,}000 replicates at $\alpha=.05$.
\emph{Panel/src} is the status quo: a paired source $t$ test on panel-majority
correctness, computable from released majority labels alone. \emph{Votes/src}
keeps the source-level standard error but uses individual votes. \emph{Rev.\
$t$} is a two-sample $t$ test on the twelve reviewer accuracies and \emph{Rev.\
RI} refers it to all $\binom{12}{6}=924$ arm assignments; the last two need
only pre-aggregation votes with reviewer identifiers, so they are executable
after collection.

\input{tables/main_retrospective_remedies}

The conclusion does not depend on choosing a value of $\sigma_r$. At
$\sigma_r=0$ the status quo is correctly calibrated ($4.7\%$ at 80 sources), so
the failure is not an artifact of the model. At our own three-coder estimate
($0.27$) it rejects a true null $24.2\%$ of the time at 80 sources and $36.7\%$
at 160. At the smallest of the large crossed designs ($\SigmaMin$) it is
$30.2\%$ and $41.5\%$; at the model panel ($\SigmaPanel$), $49.6\%$ and
$63.0\%$; at the largest ($\SigmaMax$), $62.6\%$ and $73.0\%$.
Adding sources makes it worse at every positive $\sigma_r$, because conditional
item precision improves while the number of prompt assignments stays at twelve.
Replacing majority labels with individual votes hurts rather than helps: the
defect is the unit over which uncertainty is computed, not the aggregation,
which is why advice to release pre-adjudication labels is necessary but not
sufficient. Both reviewer-level analyses hold the nominal level across the
entire grid, with observed size between $3.7\%$ and $5.7\%$
(Figure~\ref{fig:remedy-calibration}). But their power falls as reviewers
become more heterogeneous, from $52.8\%$ at $\sigma_r=0.27$ to $11.4\%$ at
$\SigmaMax$: a twelve-reviewer two-arm study is underpowered at realistic
reviewer heterogeneity. The status quo appears to retain power only because it rejects
far too often under the null. The remedy is more reviewers, not a smaller
standard error over the wrong unit.

\subsection{A pre-collection checklist}
\label{sec:practical-audit}

Five decisions follow (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:robust-rag-reporting-checklist}). \emph{Name the claim and
decision}: verifying source support, deciding comparability and recommending an
action are three targets. \emph{Draw the unit map}: if prompts go to people,
people remain the treatment units after majority voting.
\emph{Construct the target independently} of the tested intervention.
\emph{Power the randomized units}: at the anchored $\sigma_r$, twelve reviewers
are badly underpowered \citep{card2020power}---the reviewer-level tests we
recommend hold their nominal size across the whole grid, but their power falls
to $11.4\%$ at $\SigmaMax$, so switching to them without also recruiting more
reviewers buys correctness and not detection. \emph{Preserve the measurement
path}: release per-reviewer pre-adjudication votes.

\section{Conclusion}

Robust RAG is not a single leaderboard. Of the \LedgerStated{} source-verified
cross-paper comparisons an author actually stated, \LedgerStatedUndef{} are
undefined under a five-coordinate audit and \LedgerStatedNarrow{} narrow to a
matched operating point; in the sharpest case one attack is recorded at $1\%$
by the paper that benchmarks it and at $84.2\%$ by its own authors, on the same
dataset and retriever, because the outcome coordinate is unmatched. The rule
also points against us: our own most
dramatic number, a $45.3$-point reversal, is discarded by it, and so is the
flagship case of our own previous version, which attributed to a paper a
quotation it does not contain.

Whether that audit can be automated has a sharper answer than we expected, and
a narrower one than we first reported. Given a deterministic frame of
\FrameCandidates{} candidates and judges from three developers, agreement
splits by \emph{kind of question}. Whether a number was transported or
re-implemented is recovered reliably, under both $AC_1$ (\DecProv{}) and
$\kappa$ (\DecProvK{}). Whether the comparison survives is not (\DecVerdict{},
\DecVerdictK{}), and a fully crossed pool of \TierPanelN{} judges---three
checkpoints from each of three developers, \TierNPairs{} pairs---shows why.
Upgrading every judge to a frontier checkpoint raises verdict $\kappa$ from
\TierSubmittedVerdictK{} to \TierFrontierVerdictK{} and no further; two frontier
checkpoints from different developers reach \TierCompFFCross{}, with some pairs
below chance, while two from the same developer reach \TierCompFFSame{}. But
the five coordinates themselves are not a coding instrument:
independent judges code them at $\kappa$ \DecCoordKMin{}--\DecCoordKMax{}, and
against human adjudication they lose to a majority-class baseline. Reading a
comparison is reproducible; adjudicating it does not port between model
families, and the configurations that agree best are the ones that share
training data---which is not the same as being right. That is why the
coordinates are fields an author should disclose rather than a score anyone can
compute, and why three released verifiers now check every quotation, every
quoted value and every number this paper asserts about its own artifacts---while
stating, because the distinction is the paper's subject, that they establish
fidelity and not correctness.

Deciding such claims with human reviewers is itself a measurement problem, and
the field cannot check its own answers: none of the nine robust-RAG papers that
collect original human judgments reports the item--annotator assignment or
releases pre-aggregation votes. The price depends on $\sigma_r$, which is why
we no longer report it from three coders alone: five public crossed human
corpora and a fully crossed machine panel place it between \SigmaMin{} and
\SigmaMax{}, where the analysis those papers make possible rejects a true null
between $\SimMinSize\%$ and $62.6\%$ of the time at 80 sources and worsens as
items accumulate, while two reviewer-level analyses hold the nominal level.
Releasing per-reviewer votes is the highest-value change available and costs
nothing; powering the reviewer dimension is second and is not free.

\section*{Limitations}

The twelve worked comparisons are cases, not a census, and the ledger has been
corrected once. An earlier version's flagship entry attributed a quotation to a
paper that does not contain it, misassigned the number to a different attack,
and treated a re-implementation as a transported claim; the corrected entry is
in the released ledger with an explicit erratum field, its verdict changed from
\texttt{narrows} to \texttt{undefined}, and the released verifier was extended
to test quoted strings and not only numerals. That the old verifier passed
while the prose was wrong is a coverage boundary we did not disclose, and it is
the paper's own indictment turned inward. Both sides of a case had to be
present in local full text with an explicit number, which favors open-access and
widely cited work; the coordinate-matched re-reading exists only where a paper
reports an operating point at the other side's setting; and the coding is ours
and single-coded, though every quoted value and quoted string is now
machine-verified against its source line.

The headline is stated at the unit that supports it. We report
\LedgerStatedUndef{} undefined among \LedgerStated{} \emph{author-stated}
comparisons, separately from \LedgerStatedNarrow{} that narrow and from
\LedgerInvited{} survey-invited juxtapositions no author made. An earlier
version reported ``ten of twelve do not survive'', which counted narrowing---a
successful recovery under our own rule---as a failure, and counted comparisons
nobody stated. $n=\LedgerStated{}$ remains thin, author-selected and
single-coded, which is why \S\ref{sec:census} carries the prevalence question
and why no numerical claim rests on the ledger's rate.

The census removes author selection but introduces its own limits. The frame is
a deterministic screen over open-access English full texts with a fixed
gazetteer and three declared strata, so it surfaces comparisons that fit those
shapes and misses those spread over a longer argument; the \FrameCandidates{}
candidates are a frame, not a sample of the literature. Its judges are language
models, not humans, so the agreement decomposition is a statement about
automated auditing rather than about people. Reporting both $AC_1$ and $\kappa$
changed one of our conclusions: the eligibility gap we previously reported does
not survive $\kappa$, and only the provenance gap does. Because judges disagree
on verdicts, no verdict-based rate from the census should be read as an
estimate, including the higher failure rate of transported comparisons: that
stratum spans only \ProvTransPapers{} papers, one contributing
\ProvTransLargest\% of it, its paper-clustered interval is \ProvTransFailCI\%,
and a permutation test at the source-paper unit gives $p=.064$. We report it as
a direction. \CensusQuoteRate\% of evidence quotes were located verbatim in the
passage shown; the remainder are paraphrases we did not attempt to repair.

The tier control identifies capability tier against developer, but not against
everything else. Two checkpoints from one developer share training data and
alignment procedure, so their agreement is an upper reference level rather than
a measure of how well the task can be done; it bounds what is achievable by
judges that are not independent, and we do not read it as evidence that a
well-specified verdict is attainable. Nine judges give \TierNPairs{} pairs,
which is enough to separate the small--small and frontier--frontier ends but not
enough to resolve the developer contrast (\TierDevDiff{}, $p=\TierDevP$); we
report it as residual structure. ``Frontier'' and ``small'' are the developers'
own tiering, not a measured capability, and the OpenAI frontier cell is served
over a different transport than the other judges---the same prompt and codebook
through the vendor CLI rather than the local proxy, because the proxy cannot
pass the parameters those checkpoints require. All nine judges are language
models, so nothing here establishes what a panel of people would do.

The nine-paper reporting audit is a single-coded, model-assisted pass over 39
venue-linked local full texts. It is neither a scoping review conducted to
protocol \citep{prismaScR2018} nor a risk-of-bias assessment
\citep{whiting2011quadas}, and the under-reporting phenomenon is established at
larger scale elsewhere \citep{howcroft2020twenty,vanderlee2021human}. Absence
of a reported field is not evidence of an absent practice.

Our own reviewer-heterogeneity estimate rests on three coders and 80 claims and
is therefore uninformative on its own. The additional anchors are real crossed
designs at scale, but the human ones are not claim review---they are
offensiveness, toxicity, sentiment, emotion and preference judgments---and each
annotator is scored against a leave-one-out majority rather than an independent
adjudication, which attenuates individual accuracies and therefore, if
anything, understates $\sigma_r$. The model panel is a fully crossed design
with a deterministic reference, but model reviewers are not human reviewers. We
draw only the weak conclusion the anchors support: $\sigma_r=0$ is not a live
possibility, and the type-I inflation is reported across the whole anchored
range rather than at a point. The simulation itself uses binary correctness, a
common wrong-label state, balanced three-of-six panels and a logit
random-effects model; it demonstrates a design failure mode and the analyses
that repair it, not a prevalence, and the two remedies are less efficient than
a crossed mixed-effects model where reviewers rate disjoint item sets.

The controlled benchmark is templated arithmetic prose with a synthetic loss
rule, and results do not predict behavior on natural paper claims. The model
sample now spans \CMJdevelopers{} developers, but it is still a convenience
sample, and its result is weak: the capability effect halves relative to the earlier
nine-checkpoint sample, its prediction interval and its developer-level
interval both contain zero, the utility effect is null, and paraphrase sign
agreement is at or below $0.5$ for \CMJSignBelowHalf\% of model-by-stratum
cells. A reader should treat neither the earlier positive result nor this one
as settling whether naming a coordinate changes a model's decision. The
API-served models were given a larger output-token budget than the open
checkpoints and required a decision normalisation; the normalisation is applied
symmetrically and changes \CMJnormOpenChanged{} of the open checkpoints'
decisions, but the two serving paths are not identical harnesses. The v1
benchmark was corrected after outcomes were observed; v2 was rebuilt and every
reported model rerun, so the v2 contrast is a fresh measurement but the
benchmark family is not preregistered.

The 80-claim diagnostic is purposive, overlaps codebook development and lacks a
recoverable selection procedure for 64 items; its result is negative and is
reported as such. The excluded 480-claim collection lacks the internal
provenance needed for a human-study claim, and no quantity derived from it
appears anywhere in this paper. Local hashes authenticate bytes, not behavioral
independence or external preregistration. No consequence-based human effect
size is estimated.

\section*{Ethics Statement}

The intended human study of this paper was a 480-claim collection, and it
failed an internal integrity check. One returned batch contained shared
response payloads, and a later export could not be linked to controlled
recruitment records, one-to-one pseudonym mappings, and separate response
receipts. Because that internal audit trail was absent, every response,
contrast, reference statistic, participant attribute, and timing claim derived
from it is excluded, including from the reviewer-heterogeneity estimate in
\S\ref{sec:simulation}; a privacy-safe invalidation receipt preserves the
shared-payload finding and the original return-manifest hash. Shared payloads
across a returned crowdwork batch is a data-integrity incident and not merely a
provenance gap: we treat the batch as unusable regardless of whether the cause
was vendor-side duplication or a collection fault, we did not re-contract the
vendor, and we make no human-subject, recruitment, expertise, compensation or
independence claim for that collection.

What remained was an 80-claim, four-contributor sample whose only public
provenance is the project owner's attestation---that all four contributors
consented to paper use and public release of de-identified annotations, were
compensated, and did not use generative AI---together with a reported
institutional determination that formal review was not required. An earlier
version of this paper reported that sample as a diagnostic subsection while
stating that a reader who declined the attestation should read the section as
reporting nothing. That is not a reasonable thing to ask of a venue, and the
tension it creates cannot be resolved by wording: a paper arguing that a
declaration is not auditable evidence cannot rest a section on one. We have
therefore removed the subsection. Two figures from that sample survive, both
chosen because they are load bearing \emph{against} our own proposal or against
our own confidence: the majority-class baseline that the five coordinates fail
to beat (\S\ref{sec:census}), and the three-coder $\hat\sigma_r=0.27$ whose
interval contains zero and which we present only to show that a three-rater
design cannot identify $\sigma_r$ (\S\ref{sec:sigma}). No positive claim in
this paper depends on either, and $\sigma_r$ is anchored on designs whose data
are public and independently re-analysable.

We distinguish public de-identification from private auditability: consent,
recruitment, and identity-linkage records may remain access-controlled, but an
empirical claim still needs an inspectable internal provenance path. The
published-paper audit uses public scholarly documents and introduces no
participant contact. The comparability cases quote short passages from
published papers for verification purposes only, and every such quotation is
checked verbatim against its cited line range by the released verifier. LLMs
assisted documentary discovery, extraction, and audit screening; those outputs
are not human gold.
The synthetic benchmark contains no participant data or third-party
quotation. The reported open-model runs use deterministic decoding and release
raw outputs. No attack payloads or poisoned corpora are released.
